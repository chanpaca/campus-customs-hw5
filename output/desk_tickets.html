Reflection

What the Campus Customs desk learned from running tickets 101, 102 and 103 to completion on 2026-08-31.

The headline is not that three tickets closed. It is that closing them consumed 95.3% of the shop's cash in a single day. We opened at $3,400.00, disbursed $3,240.00 across two approvals, and closed at $160.00 — and both of those payments were correct, overdue or imminent, and properly authorised. There is no mistake to point at. That is precisely what makes it instructive: a desk can execute flawlessly and still end the day one obligation away from insolvency.

The $3,400 → $160 move is the lens for everything below. It is why ticket 102's sequencing note mattered more than any individual lookup, why ticket 103's $0.00 cash impact was the right outcome rather than a missed sale, and why the shop now holds $160.00 in cash against $3,016.00 of inventory at list — 32 navy hoodies, 40 caps and 10 tees — with rent of $2,400.00 due again in roughly a month. Cash on hand covers 6.7% of that obligation. The desk has to convert stock to cash, and it has about four weeks to do it.

Read this before the performance section. The Portkey key for gpt-6-luna is over its usage limit (HTTP 412), so no model ever ran. The tool calls, database writes, approvals and cash figures below are real; the routing decisions were executed by backend/scripted_run.py following the Expected plans. So this is an honest evaluation of the harness — the tools, the prompts as written, the guardrails and the hand-off structure — and not of model judgment. Where a conclusion would require a model to have actually chosen something, it is marked untested.

1 · Agent performance across the three tickets

Inventory — the strongest showing

Inventory's defining moment was ticket 101. Asked "can we fill 1 × CC-TEE-WHITE size S today", the obvious answer is "no, we are out". What it actually returned was qty_on_hand: 0 and the observation that the ticket carries invoice_id=501, so the restock already exists and the question is whether the vendor has been paid. That reframing is what turned a stock ticket into a cash ticket and set up the entire chain. On ticket 103 it did the quieter version of the same job: reporting the per-size spread S 4 · M 8 · L 14 · XL 6 and stating explicitly that no other size substitutes for a size-M order — the difference between "we have 32 hoodies" and "we have 8 of what they asked for."

Credit where it is due, though: that detection is largely designed into the tool, not discovered by the agent. get_vendor_invoices returns linked tickets with their live qty_on_hand in the same response, so the stockout and the unpaid bill arrive together whether or not anyone is clever. The prompt file reinforces it; the data shape does the heavy lifting.

Facilities — narrow scope, exactly right

Facilities touched only ticket 102 and did one thing well: it refused to treat the email as the obligation. The ticket note reads "Email: shop rent due in 2 days." Facilities pulled lease 1 and confirmed $2,400.00 to Elm City Properties for the Chapel Street shop, next due 2026-09-02, 2 days out, then paid the lease's figure. On this run the email happened to agree with the lease, so the control was never truly stressed — the interesting test (an email demanding $2,600, or naming a different landlord) has not been run. Untested: whether a model would have checked rather than simply paid.

Accounting — the busiest agent, and the one carrying the control

Accounting was the only agent to appear on all three tickets, and the only one that ever touches money. On 101 it established invoice 501 at $840.00, 3 days past due against a $3,400.00 balance; on 102 it confirmed $2,560.00 remained and flagged that a third disbursement would not fit; on 103 it set the floor at $22.00/unit and capped the discount at 62.1% off the $58.00 list. In every case it drafted and stopped. It never called record_approved_payment, and when an earlier test had it try, the tool refused: "'Accounting' is an agent, not a human approver."

Customer Service — did the hardest thing, which was not promising

On 101 it had every incentive to tell Tauhid Zaman "soon". It quoted 2026-09-05, which is the desk date plus Bulldog Print Co's lead_days: 5, and attached the condition that the vendor had to be paid first. On 103 it drafted "8 now, 12 to follow" rather than rounding a 12-unit shortfall into reassurance, and marked the discount as proposed rather than granted. It also ran the only double peer hand-off of the run, pulling in Inventory and Accounting itself.

Boss — correctly idle

One outbound call per ticket, then out of the way: Inventory for 101 (the ticket carries sku/size/qty), Facilities for 102 (lease_id), Customer Service for 103 (the deliverable is a message). Five of eight hand-offs happened without it. The anti-pattern the prompt warns against — "Boss calls everyone" — did not occur. Untested: the routing was scripted, so what this really validates is that the ticket schema carries enough signal to route on (invoice_id, lease_id, sku), not that a model would read it correctly.

Coordination bottlenecks

The binding constraint on this desk is infrastructure, not intelligence. Each of the 19 MCP tool calls across the three tickets spawns a fresh server subprocess costing 3–5 seconds, which means the plumbing — not the thinking — sets the wall-clock cost of a ticket. A persistent MCP session would compress the run by roughly an order of magnitude, and it is the single highest-return engineering fix available. Hand-offs compound the problem by serialising: on ticket 103, Customer Service asked Inventory about reprint lead times and Accounting about the margin floor, two questions where neither answer depends on the other, and ran them one after the other anyway.

The structural concern is concentration. Accounting appears in all three chains and is the only agent that can draft a voucher, which makes it both the busiest node and a single point of failure for every ticket that touches money. On a day when the shop moved $3,240.00, that is the role worth hardening first — a slow or wrong Accounting agent does not degrade the desk gracefully, it stalls the cash pipeline entirely. The team also re-fetches rather than shares: get_inventory_and_pricing was called three times on ticket 103 alone, by three different agents, for overlapping data on the same SKU. Findings travel as questions, not as context.

One control went unexercised. Peak delegation usage was 3 turns against a budget of 10, so the shared ceiling that exists in code was never approached, let alone stressed. We know the budget is enforced; we do not know how the team behaves when it runs out.

Prompt nuances worth recording

Three design choices in the prompt files did disproportionate work, and each one guards a specific failure that would have cost money. The (sku, size) warning in inventory.md, reinforced by the total_units_all_sizes field description in the tool schema, exists to prevent one error: reading 32 when the answer is 8. Had that fired on ticket 103, the shop would have committed to 20 hoodies it could not ship, against a balance of $160.00 with no capacity to expedite. The framing in facilities.md — "the lease is the fact, the email is a claim" — is the only reason ticket 102 contains a verification step at all; without it, the shortest path is to pay whatever the message demands, which is how a shop with $2,560.00 on hand wires it to the wrong party. And every prompt closing with "what you produce", paired with the typed AgentOutput, is what makes a cited fact traceable to the tool that produced it rather than to a confident sentence.

Untested, and worth saying plainly: none of these were exercised by a model. They are well-formed instructions whose effect on behaviour remains unmeasured, and treating them as proven controls would be exactly the overconfidence they were written to prevent.

3 · Single-agent vs multi-agent, judged on this run

The case for one monolithic agent

On the evidence of this run, a single agent holding all eight tools would have been the better operational choice, and it is worth being honest about why. Ticket 101 took three hand-offs, each serialising a model call behind the one before it, on top of the 3–5 seconds every MCP subprocess costs. A monolith answers the same ticket in one pass over the same data. It would also stop paying for work twice: ticket 103 queried get_inventory_and_pricing three times because each agent starts cold, where one agent reads the SKU once and carries it. The token economics point the same direction — five system prompts and a hand-off question restating facts the caller already had, versus one prompt and one context.

The maintenance burden follows the same logic. A meaningful share of backend/agents.py — the delegation tools, the shared budget accounting, the depth limits, the DeskDeps plumbing — exists solely because there is more than one agent. And the single genuine deviation from plan in this entire run was an ordering question between Accounting and Customer Service on ticket 101. A monolith cannot have that class of bug, because there is no seam for it to live in.

What the multi-agent structure uniquely provided

Against that, the architecture bought one thing this run proves is worth having: segregation of financial duties on a day when 95.3% of the shop's cash went out the door. Inventory found the problem, Accounting priced it, and a human authorised it. No single actor spanned detection, preparation and authorisation. Both rows in the ledger read approved_by: "Chanseo Park"; no agent name appears anywhere in payments. A monolith carrying record_approved_payment in the same toolbelt as the lookups is one bad inference away from disbursing $840.00 on its own authority — the blocklist would still refuse an agent name, but the architecture would no longer be the thing preventing it, and when the balance is $160.00 the margin for a bad inference is gone.

The second benefit is that domain prompts can afford to be specific. The (sku, size) trap gets real space in inventory.md; the cost floor gets real space in accounting.md; the email-versus-lease distinction gets real space in facilities.md. Merged into one system prompt, each warning competes for attention with four other roles' rules, and the guidance that caught the 12-unit shortfall on ticket 103 becomes one line in a wall of text. Scoping tools per role has the same effect on blast radius: Inventory and Customer Service literally cannot draft a voucher, so a misbehaving agent is bounded by its job description.

Third, and most valuable to a manager after the fact, the audit trail reads as a conversation rather than a log. The 55 events from this run record who asked whom what — "Inventory → Accounting: size S is at 0 and invoice 501 looks unpaid, please check and prepare payment". A monolith produces a flat sequence of tool calls, and reconstructing why it checked the invoice means inferring intent after the fact. When the question is "explain this $3,240.00 outflow", the hand-off record is the explanation.

The managerial verdict. At three tickets a day, a single well-prompted agent with the same eight tools and the same human-approval gate would be faster, cheaper and very nearly as safe — because the hard control is record_approved_payment refusing agent approvers, and that lives in the MCP server, not in the agent topology. The multi-agent design earns its cost at the point where prompts start colliding and where "who decided this?" must be answerable from the record. Campus Customs is not there yet. But the day the shop runs its balance down to $160.00 is also the day segregation of duties stops being an abstraction, so I would keep the structure and spend the engineering budget on a persistent MCP session instead — buying the latency back without giving up the control.

4 · Three problems this team could solve today, with the tools it already has

a. The Crest Mug has been at zero and nobody has noticed

CC-MUG-CREST / OS sits at qty 0 in Aisle D right now — a stockout with no ticket attached, found only because get_inventory_and_pricing returns every size. A reorder-threshold sweep needs nothing new: call get_inventory_and_pricing across the four SKUs, flag anything at or below a floor (mugs at 0, CC-TEE-WHITE / XL at 2, CC-HOOD-NAVY / S at 4), check affordability with get_cash_balance, and have Accounting draft vouchers for a human. Elm City Gifts carries lead_days: 3 for mugs and small goods, so a reorder placed on the desk date lands around 2026-09-03. The one caveat the run itself proves: with $160.00 left after the $3,400 → $160 drawdown, almost any reorder is unaffordable today — a mug restock at $3.50 a unit is one of the few the shop could still fund. That constraint is exactly what the sweep should surface, because the alternative is discovering it at the moment a customer asks.

b. Re-routing when the Bulldog reprint slips

Ticket 101's customer was quoted 2026-09-05 off Bulldog Print Co's lead_days: 5. If that slips, the team already has what it needs to respond: get_vendor_invoices exposes every vendor with its lead time, and QuickShip CT carries lead_days: 1. Inventory compares lead times, Accounting checks whether a courier charge fits the remaining balance, and Customer Service re-quotes a date drawn from a vendor record rather than an apology. No new tool — this is the existing three-agent chain from ticket 101 pointed at a different question.

c. A forward obligation calendar — and a gap this run exposed

With get_cash_balance, get_vendor_invoices and get_lease_details, the team can project what is owed against what is left: $160.00 in checking against a $2,400.00 monthly rent means roughly $2,240.00 has to arrive before the next cycle. Cash on hand covers 6.7% of that rent; the shop's $3,016.00 of inventory at list — 32 hoodies, 40 caps, 10 tees — is the only thing standing between the desk and a missed payment, which makes sell-through the operating priority rather than a reporting nicety. Running that projection surfaced a real defect: record_approved_payment settles the rent but never advances leases.next_due, which still reads 2026-09-02 even though payment id 2 cleared it. Any forward-looking report would double-count September's rent. That is a fix inside an existing tool, not a new one — and the kind of thing a recurring obligations check exists to catch.

5 · Three problems this team cannot solve, and what each would require

a. "Can you put our club crest on 40 hoodies?" — custom embroidery approval

The Yale AI Club's next request after ticket 103 is the obvious one, and the desk is helpless. The database has no artwork, no customisation table, no setup fees, and no concept of a design at all — pricing holds exactly two numbers per SKU (unit_cost, list_price), neither of which survives contact with a $45 digitising charge or a per-stitch rate. Worse, nothing in the system can judge whether a design is permissible: Yale's trademarks are licensed, and approving a crest is a legal question, not an inventory one.

Would require: an artwork / customisation schema with setup fees and per-unit decoration costs; a vision-capable tool to check a mockup against brand rules; an integration with Yale's trademark licensing office for approval status; and a Brand Compliance agent whose job is to say no. The human-approval gate would need a second kind of approval — design sign-off — distinct from a cash disbursement.

b. "Get us a better price than $22.00 a hoodie" — competitive supplier bidding

Ticket 103 hinged on a $22.00 unit cost that the system treats as a law of nature. It is a single number in a single row, with no history, no quantity breaks, and no alternative source. The vendors table holds three rows with a name, a specialty and a lead time — no prices, no terms, no contact details, no contract. The desk cannot ask Bulldog Print Co for a volume quote, cannot compare it to another printer, and has no way to send a message to anyone: every tool reads or writes SQLite, and the team has no outbound channel whatsoever.

Would require: vendor contact and contract records with quantity-break pricing and quote history; an outbound communication tool (email or a supplier portal API); an inbound parser to read quotes back; a negotiation policy with authority limits; and a Procurement agent. This is also where the human-in-the-loop boundary has to move — committing the shop to a supplier contract is a bigger decision than approving $840.00 against an invoice that already exists.

c. "I never got my tee and I'm disputing the charge" — chargeback arbitration

The sharpest gap this project has. Follow ticket 101's customer: Tauhid Zaman is waiting on a tee quoted for 2026-09-05. If he paid and disputes the charge, the desk cannot respond at all. There is no customers table, no orders table, and no record that ticket 101 represents a sale — the ticket is the only trace, and it has now been marked resolved. The payments table is outflow-only: every row is money leaving for a vendor or a landlord. There is no inbound payment, no refund, no reversal concept, and record_approved_payment would reject a negative amount as a mismatch against the obligation.

Would require: customer and order tables linking a person to a payment and a fulfilment event; a payment-processor integration (Stripe or similar) to receive dispute notifications and submit evidence; refund and reversal support in the ledger, with the double-entry care that implies; deadline tracking, since processors give fixed windows to respond; and a Disputes agent with an escalation path to a human who carries real financial authority. A chargeback is adversarial and time-boxed — the opposite of the calm, internally-verifiable work this desk is built for. It is also the gap with the sharpest financial teeth: a shop holding $160.00 after a $3,240.00 day cannot absorb a disputed charge plus a processor fee, and would have no evidence trail to contest it with.

What the run actually proved

The cash reconciled exactly: $3,400.00 − $840.00 − $2,400.00 − $0.00 = $160.00, two payment rows totalling $3,240.00, invoice 501 marked paid, all three tickets resolved, and every approved_by naming a human. The guardrails refused an agent approver, a wrong amount, a double payment and an overdraft when tested directly. The tools return real rows and say found: false rather than guessing.

What it did not prove is whether a language model, given these prompts and these tools, makes these choices. That question stays open until the quota resets — and it is the one question a reflection on agent performance would most like to answer.
